Course description

Calendar description

LING_V 518 (3–9) Advanced Research Seminar. This course is not eligible for Credit/D/Fail grading. This section is offered for 3 credits.

Extended description

LING 518 is a graduate course on quantitative methods in linguistics. It will provide you with a thorough grounding in quantitative data analysis, covering the entire process between data collection and publication. Unlike many other statistics courses, there is a heavy emphasis on practical skills in data wrangling, visualisation, the presentation of quantitative results, and open science alongside more conventional topics such as descriptive and inferential statistics.

The course will be taught with a heavy focus on discussion and practice. Classes will take specific data and problems as their starting point, and theoretical concepts will be discussed as needed, rather than the other way around. As a result, the course will proceed naturally at a pace that we set ourselves, placing more emphasis where needed and tackling easier concepts in a shorter timeframe.

Readings and additional materials such as slides will be introduced on an as-needed basis, typically during or after classes rather than being set far in advance. Canvas will be used to share these materials.

In statistical modelling, we cover simple regression models and more advanced topics such as mixed-effects regression and logistic regression. The course also includes an extended block on Bayesian statistics, showing you how to fit, interpret, and evaluate simple and more complex regression models in a Bayesian framework. You will also have the opportunity to suggest further topics for discussion.

The main goal is not simply to step through a long list of statistical methods, but to provide a deeper understanding of the principles behind them and the connections among them. We will focus on the often straightforward intuitions behind statistical methods, not on mathematical formulae. By the end of the course, you will have the skills not only to run your own analyses, but also to think critically about statistical analysis and continue developing your understanding without supervision from an instructor.

Course delivery and learning activities

The Tuesday and Thursday sessions follow the progression outlined in “Course content and schedule.” There is no dedicated lecture versus lab time: each session combines short lecture-style explanations, demonstrations, discussion, practical exercises, and substantial hands-on work in R.

A key aspect of the course is using R. R is a statistical software environment that has become a de facto standard in linguistics and the social sciences. It can be daunting at first because it involves coding, but it is worth persisting through the initial learning curve. R enables you to create publication-quality graphics, wrangle and combine data sets, conduct reproducible research, and use a very broad range of statistical tools.

Some sessions will also involve Bring Your Own Data activities, short “Show and Tell” presentations, or student-led discussions of advanced readings. Readings supplement the in-class work; unless Canvas says otherwise, you should complete assigned readings before the relevant class.

The final letter grade will follow UBC’s Grading Practices.

Data exercises (4 × 10% = 40%)

Four data-oriented exercises test skills acquired in the course: data wrangling, data visualisation, regression modelling, and Bayesian data analysis. For each exercise, you will receive a data set and have one full week to complete a short set of tasks drawing on skills practised in class. You will submit an R Markdown document that explains your solution step by step. A grading rubric will be published on Canvas with each exercise.

Final data analysis project (45%)

This project is the culmination of the course. You will take either your own data set or a suitable public data set and conduct a thorough exploratory or confirmatory analysis. The project must:

  • include at least one well-designed plot that is an organic part of the analysis;
  • use at least one exploratory or confirmatory technique discussed in the course, or another technique you can use confidently;
  • clearly motivate analytical decisions and demonstrate awareness of key statistical issues; and
  • describe all stages of the analysis, including hypotheses or exploratory aims, data wrangling, visualisation, modelling, interpretation, and conclusions.

Projects may differ widely across linguistic subfields. The aim is not to fit a single mould, but to demonstrate a critical and creative approach to quantitative analysis. The write-up will take the form of a concise, reproducible “lab report” in R Markdown, including code, graphs, and model output. It is not an essay or polished journal article; clear bullet points and other informal devices are acceptable when justified, though full sentences are expected.

This assessment is designed to support your own research. You are encouraged to choose a topic and data set that connect to a qualifying paper, MA thesis, PhD dissertation, or independent research project. If the project forms part of your program research, consult your committee about what is suitable. You are also encouraged to meet with the instructor before settling on a project plan.

Homework (10%)

Regular brief homework exercises are formative: they are not graded for quality, but they are compulsory and must be submitted. Many focus on R coding or the application of specific techniques discussed in class.

Participation (5%)

You can earn the full participation mark by completing any one of the following options; performance is not graded.

  1. Bring Your Own Data (BYOD): provide a data set for an in-class “data surgery,” together with a one- or two-paragraph description of the data, key variables, and questions, at least one week before the session. The data will be shared with the class for collective analysis and discussion.
  2. Show and Tell: give a 10-minute mini-presentation on a statistical technique or problem from your own work that is likely to interest the class. Discuss the topic with the instructor in advance.
  3. Lead a discussion: engage closely with an assigned advanced reading, briefly summarise it, and prepare discussion points for a 15–20-minute class discussion.
Learning materials
  • Software: R and RStudio. Both are free and available for Windows, macOS, and Linux. Installation guidance will be provided on Canvas.
  • Course files: slides, data sets, assignment materials, and selected readings will be provided through Canvas at no additional cost.
  • Readings: all required readings are available through the UBC Library, freely online, or as PDFs on Canvas. There is no required textbook purchase.
  • Computer access: you will need regular access to a computer capable of running R and RStudio. Contact the instructor promptly if this creates an access barrier.

The course draws principally on Winter (2019), Wickham and Grolemund (2016), and selected tutorials and journal articles. Readings are introduced as needed and listed in Canvas.

Course policies

Attendance

Regular attendance at Tuesday and Thursday meetings is expected. Because the course emphasises discussion, demonstrations, and hands-on practice, absences may make it difficult to meet the course goals. If you must miss class for circumstances covered by academic concession, contact the instructor as early as reasonably possible so that an appropriate plan can be discussed.

Academic integrity and misconduct

Academic integrity in LING 518A means that submitted work accurately represents your own understanding, analysis, and code. Unless an assignment says otherwise, you may discuss ideas and debugging strategies with classmates, but your submission must be independently produced. Acknowledge all sources, reused code, substantial assistance, and tools. Direct quotations must be clearly marked and attributed; presenting another person’s words, ideas, analysis, or code as your own is plagiarism.

Suspected academic misconduct must be handled under UBC procedures and may be reported to the Department and Faculty. Review the Academic Calendar’s statement on academic misconduct and the Academic Integrity for Students resource.

Use of generative AI

Generative AI tools may be used in this course for brainstorming, clarifying concepts, improving prose, and obtaining coding or debugging suggestions, unless a particular assessment states otherwise. Any substantive use must be disclosed in the submitted work, including the tool used, what it was used for, and how its output was checked or revised. You remain responsible for the accuracy, originality, reproducibility, and interpretation of everything you submit.

Generative AI may not replace the intellectual work being assessed: do not submit AI-generated analyses, interpretations, explanations, code, or prose as though they were your own, and do not enter confidential, restricted, or identifiable research data into an external AI service. These limits ensure that your work demonstrates the statistical reasoning, coding, interpretation, and communication skills that the course is designed to develop. Assignment-specific instructions take precedence over this general policy.

Academic concession, missed work, and late submission

Academic concessions support students experiencing unexpected or short-term circumstances that interfere with academic work. Requests should be made in writing as early as reasonably possible. For a short-term issue affecting one item, contact the instructor promptly. If the disruption is prolonged, repeated, affects several courses, or may require a change to your academic record, contact your graduate advisor or home Faculty’s academic advising office. See UBC’s Academic Concession policy.

When there is a serious and legitimate reason for missing work, the instructor will discuss an appropriate in-term concession, which may include an extension, alternative task, or adjusted deadline. Contact the instructor before the deadline whenever possible. Late summative work without an approved extension is penalised by 10% of the available mark per calendar day. Late formative homework is generally not accepted. If you submit work in Canvas after a deadline, notify the instructor because marking may already have begun.

Accessibility and accommodation

UBC’s Centre for Accessibility coordinates academic accommodations for students with disabilities and ongoing medical conditions. If you have an accommodation letter, provide it to the instructor as early as possible so the required arrangements can be implemented. Students whose religious, spiritual, or cultural observances conflict with course requirements should contact the instructor in advance. See UBC’s information on academic accommodations for students with disabilities and ongoing medical conditions.

Announcements and communication

Important announcements will be made through Canvas. You are responsible for reading them and following the instructions they contain. Monitor your UBC email so that you do not miss course messages.

University policies and resources

UBC provides resources to support student learning and to maintain healthy lifestyles but recognizes that sometimes crises arise and so there are additional resources to access including those for survivors of sexual assault. UBC values respect for the person and ideas of all members of the academic community. Harassment and discrimination are not tolerated nor is suppression of academic freedom. UBC provides appropriate accommodation for students with disabilities and for religious, spiritual and cultural observances. UBC values academic honesty and students are expected to acknowledge the ideas generated by others and to uphold the highest academic standards in all of their actions.

Details of the policies and how to access support are available on the UBC Senate website.
